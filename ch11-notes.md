# Chapter 11 — "Don't Just Buy What You Know"
*Notes and analysis (not book text). A focused extension of the "young companies" theme from Ch. 3 — specific rules for evaluating recently-listed stocks. Narrower scope than earlier chapters; mostly reinforces existing rules with IPO-specific numeric bounds.*

## The "primary base" concept
- **Definition**: the first genuinely buyable base/consolidation a stock forms after going public — a corrective period of at least ~3 weeks, followed by a breakout to a new all-time high (or a solid consolidation near the all-time high).
- **Timing stat**: consistent with Ch. 3 — a large share of major market winners historically were IPOs within roughly the prior 8 years; the biggest growth phase typically happens in a company's first 5–10 years as a public company.

## Concrete quality bounds for a primary base (IPO-specific numeric rules)
- **Minimum base duration**: ~3–5 weeks before it should be considered reliable.
- **Short base (≈3 weeks) correction ceiling**: should not exceed **~25%**.
- **Longer bases (up to ~1 year)** can correct as much as **~50%** and still be considered sound.
- **Anti-example worth encoding as a rejection rule**: a new listing that corrects sharply (the book's example: -43% within 12 days) *without* first forming any real base at all should be excluded — a fast, un-based decline right after listing is a disqualifying pattern, not a "wait and see."
- Multiple valid buy points can occur in sequence for the same young stock (primary base, then a second-stage base weeks later) — same "bases 1–2 are the best, favor early bases" logic as Ch. 5, just applied specifically to recent IPOs.

## Two reinforcing risk reminders (not new information, but worth repeating here since this chapter pairs directly with the risk-management chapters next)
- A valid primary base is still **not a guarantee** — it must be paired with a firm stop-loss/exit plan regardless of how good the setup looks. Same discipline as everywhere else in the strategy.
- **Avoid "official growth stock" / institutional-favorite names that are already obvious and over-owned** (same anti-pattern as Ch. 6's institutional-favorite category) — by the time a stock is a widely recognized blue-chip growth name, its best growth phase is usually already behind it. Prefer newer, still-undiscovered names forming primary bases over already-famous names.
