# Chapter 6 — "Categories, Industry Groups, and Catalysts"
*Notes and analysis (not book text). Mostly a classification framework — useful for how we tag/segment the EGX universe, less about hard numeric filters.*

## The six company categories
Classifying a candidate helps set the right expectations and evaluation lens for it. Useful as a **tagging layer** on our universe, not a filter by itself:

1. **Market leader** — #1–3 in its industry by sales/earnings, gaining share. The category the author trades most and likes best. Typical earnings growth ≥20%, often 35–45%+ in its best years, sometimes triple-digit.
2. **Top competitor** — the #2/#3 player riding in the leader's draft; can still produce strong (if smaller) gains, and can eventually take the #1 spot.
3. **Institutional favorite** — mature "blue chip" names (low-to-mid-teens earnings growth). Safe, but rarely produces superperformance-scale moves — usually already too big/widely followed for a big % gain.
4. **Turnaround situation** — a struggling company inflecting upward. Concrete numeric marker: **2–3 consecutive quarters of strong earnings growth, or one quarter strong enough to push trailing-12-month EPS back to/above its old peak.** Look for recent-quarter growth (often 100%+) sharply *accelerating* versus a weak/negative 3–5 year trailing growth rate — the acceleration itself is the signal, not the absolute growth number.
5. **Cyclical stock** — tied to economic/commodity cycles (autos, steel, chemicals, etc.). Behaves opposite to growth stocks on P/E — see below.
6. **Laggard** — same group as a leader, but weaker earnings/price performance, often "cheaper" on paper for real fundamental reasons. Treat as an anti-pattern, same as the broken-leader trap in Ch. 4: don't buy it just because it looks cheap relative to the group leader.

## Cyclical stocks — an explicit exception to normal P/E logic
Cyclicals have an **inverse** P/E pattern versus growth stocks:
- **Near the bottom of the cycle** (good entry window): earnings falling, dividends being cut, P/E abnormally *high*, news bad.
- **Near the top of the cycle** (time to be cautious/exit): earnings rising, dividends being raised, P/E abnormally *low*, news good.
- Practical implication: if we ever tag a stock as cyclical (autos, steel, chemicals, construction materials, shipping — relevant sectors for EGX), the normal soft-score/P/E logic should be inverted, or at minimum, cyclical stocks should be excluded from any composite that treats "high P/E" as risk and "low P/E" as opportunity.

## Industry-group dynamics — useful for a "sector strength" layer
- **Historically, over 60% of superperformance stocks were part of a broader industry-group advance.** Individual stock signals get meaningfully stronger when the stock's peers are also breaking out — this argues for a **sector/group relative-strength layer** sitting alongside the individual Trend Template.
- **A new bull market is typically led by a small number of groups** (roughly 3–4, sometimes up to 8–10 industry groups/subgroups), and those leadership groups often start moving *before* the broad market index bottoms.
- **Practical detection method described**: track which sectors have a rising concentration of stocks hitting new 52-week highs — that concentration itself is the "which groups are leading right now" signal, rather than deciding sector strength top-down from macro opinion.
- **Warning signal**: if a top 1–3 stock in a group breaks down badly after a big advance, treat it as an early warning for the rest of the group, not an isolated event.
- Historically strongest categories for superperformance broadly: consumer/retail, technology/software, drugs/medical/biotech, leisure/entertainment. (US-market historical pattern — for EGX we'd want to test which sectors have actually produced EGX's own past winners rather than assuming the same list transfers directly.)

## Retail-specific sub-metric (narrower relevance, but concrete): same-store sales
- Same-store sales (comps) growth of **~10%+ is considered healthy**; **25–30%+ is flagged as unsustainable.**
- New-store expansion pace: >100 new stores/year flagged as a real risk of overextension (Starbucks' 2006 example — stock fell 82% over the following two years after an aggressive expansion year).
- Only relevant if/when we build retail-sector-specific scoring; not a universal rule.
