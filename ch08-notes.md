# Chapter 8 — "Assessing Earnings Quality"
*Notes and analysis (not book text). This chapter is about not being fooled by headline numbers — real, sustainable earnings vs. engineered ones. Contains one of the best single composite signals in the book: "Code 33."*

## ⭐ The "Code 33" — best single composite signal in this chapter
**Three consecutive quarters of simultaneous acceleration in earnings growth, sales growth, AND profit margin.** Not just earnings accelerating, not just sales accelerating — all three moving the right direction together for 3 quarters running. Described as the strongest possible combination for fueling a real superperformance move.

**Directly implementable**: this combines our Ch. 7 earnings-acceleration and sales-acceleration flags with a third margin-acceleration flag, all required simultaneously for 3 quarters → a strong soft-score booster or even a standalone tag ("Code 33 candidate").

## Earnings quality red flags — screen out engineered numbers, don't just read the headline
- **Strip out non-recurring/one-time gains** (asset sales, legal settlements, etc.) before evaluating "earnings growth" — a headline beat can be entirely a one-off event masking a real decline in core operating earnings. Need core/adjusted EPS, not just reported EPS, where the data source allows it.
- **Repeated one-time charges are themselves a red flag** — if "one-time" items show up quarter after quarter, they're not really one-time; earnings quality should be discounted.
- **Guidance manipulation pattern**: company issues downside guidance → analysts lower estimates → company then "beats" the now-lowered bar. If a recent estimate-lowering preceded a "beat," treat the beat with suspicion rather than at face value.
- **Cost-cutting-only earnings growth is lower quality** than revenue-driven growth. Three ways a company grows profit: higher volume, higher prices, lower costs. Cost-cutting alone has a limited shelf life — the best candidates show revenue growth AND margin improvement together, not margin improvement from cuts alone.

## Margin metrics
- **Gross margin**: revenue minus cost of goods, reflects pricing power and cost control.
- **Net margin**: net income ÷ sales, reflects everything (including costs, taxes, interest).
- Want to see **margins improving consistently**, ideally compared favorably to industry peers (relative margin quality = a management-quality signal).

## Price reaction to earnings — a purely technical/price-based quality check (no extra data needed beyond price)
This is genuinely useful for us since it needs no extra fundamentals data at all — just price/volume around the earnings date. Three things to check about how a stock trades right after reporting:
1. **Initial response** — did it rally or sell off on the news?
2. **Subsequent resistance** — did it hold the gain, or get sold into over following days?
3. **Resilience** — did it recover quickly after any pullback, or fail to bounce?

**Rule of thumb**: a genuinely strong report should produce a price reaction that holds up. If a stock pops on "good" earnings but then sells off hard and can't recover, treat that as a warning sign regardless of what the headline numbers said — **the market's price reaction is the real verdict, more trustworthy than the reported numbers themselves.**
- **Post-earnings drift (PED)**: a genuine surprise's price effect can persist for months — buying isn't automatically "too late" just because the initial pop already happened.

## Guidance quality
- Look for **raised guidance accompanying a beat** — the combination (not just the beat alone) is the strong signal.
- **Guidance reversal is a severe red flag**: raising guidance and then cutting it again shortly after (the book's example: 11 trading days) signals real internal confusion or dishonesty, not just normal uncertainty.
- **Discount long-term (year+) guidance/projections** — treat as closer to "spin" than signal. Weight near-term guidance (next quarter, current fiscal year) much more heavily.

## Balance sheet checks (inventory & receivables) — data-availability caveat for us
- **Inventory growing meaningfully faster than sales** (without a clear explanation like new store openings) = warning sign, especially for depreciable/perishable goods (electronics, fashion, seasonal retail) where a glut forces future price cuts.
- **Receivables growing meaningfully faster than sales** = possible sign customers are struggling to pay / weakening demand.
- **Both together, unexplained** = "double trouble" — flagged as a stronger combined warning than either alone.
- **Caveat for Kashif**: this needs granular balance-sheet line items (inventory breakdown, receivables) which is exactly the kind of deep fundamentals data we already flagged as weak/unreliable for EGX. Treat as **optional, best-effort** — mainly usable for manufacturing/retail names if and when we get clean balance-sheet data, not a required universal check.
